You paid for your website a year ago. It works, it looks good, it shows up on Google… you think. When someone asks whether it brings in customers, the honest answer is "no idea, probably some". Which is a shame, because measuring website performance for a small business doesn't take an analyst. It takes looking at four specific numbers once a month and knowing what each one means.

Let's get into it, minus the forty-chart dashboards.

## Before you measure website performance: what you actually want to know

Before opening any tool, be clear about the question. For a local business it's almost always one of these:

- Do people find me when they search for what I do in my area?
- Of those who visit, how many call, message or book?
- Which pages work and which ones are dead weight?

Visits alone answer none of them. A thousand visits from another country leaving after five seconds are worth less than twenty from your town that end in a phone call. That's why the tools below are read together.

## Search Console for beginners: what happens before the click

Search Console is free, it's Google's own, and it tells you what happens in search results before anyone reaches your website. The "Performance" report gives you four numbers:

- Impressions: how many times your site appeared in results.
- Clicks: how many times someone clicked.
- CTR: the percentage of impressions that turned into clicks.
- Average position: where you usually appear.

The most useful part is underneath, in the list of queries: the exact searches you appear for. That's where you see whether you show up for "physio in Santander" or only for your business name (which means only people who already know you are finding you).

Two quick readings I tend to make. Lots of impressions but few clicks for a search: that page's title or description probably isn't convincing. A search you care about sitting in positions 8-15: you're close, and improving that page could lift you into the part people actually see.

## Google Analytics for small business: what happens after the click

Google Analytics (GA4) measures what visitors do on your site. It can be overwhelming, so for a local business I'd keep it to a few things:

- Where they come from: Google search, your Google listing, social media, direct links.
- Which pages they visit.
- And the important bit: key events. That's GA4's name for the actions you care about: tapping the phone number, sending the form, completing a booking, opening WhatsApp.

Those events don't come set up out of the box; you have to mark them. Without them, Analytics tells you how many people visit but not how many contact you, which is the only thing you really wanted to know.

A legal detail worth not skipping: in the EU and the UK, analytics that uses cookies normally needs the visitor's consent under data protection and privacy rules, so your cookie banner has to let people genuinely refuse. In practice that means Analytics won't see everyone; keep that in mind when reading the numbers. There are simpler cookieless analytics tools, and for many business websites they're enough.

## Your Google listing measures too

For a local business, your Google Business Profile is often the main front door, and its performance panel shows how many people called you from it, asked for directions or clicked through to your website. Plenty of people never reach the website: they see the listing, call, done. If you only look at Analytics, those customers don't exist.

A useful trick: add a campaign parameter (UTM) to the website link on your listing. That way Analytics can tell visits from your listing apart from regular search visits.

## What no tool measures

Ask. "How did you hear about us?" on the form, on the first call or when booking. It's the oldest metric, and it doesn't depend on cookies or settings. If you have a booking system, add that field and within a few months you'll have an answer no dashboard gives you as clearly.

## How often to check

Once a month is enough. Comparing with the previous month is misleading because of seasonality (August isn't November), so compare with the same month last year once you have the data. Note three things in a spreadsheet: searches you appear for, contacts from the website and calls from your listing. Six months of that sheet will tell you more about your website than any automated report.
