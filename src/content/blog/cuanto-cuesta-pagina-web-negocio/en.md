You ask for three quotes for your business website and get back three numbers that look nothing alike. One is the price of a dinner out, another looks like a second-hand car, and the third sits somewhere in between with no explanation. No wonder you're asking how much a small business website should actually cost. The honest answer is that it depends, and the useful part is knowing what it depends on, because then you can read any quote with a critical eye.

I'm only talking about business websites here: the ones that say what you do, where you are and how to contact you or book. An online store or an app is a different league, with its own maths.

## How much does a small business website cost? What drives the price

When someone quotes for a website, they're really estimating hours. Website design cost comes from those hours, and the hours come from here:

- How many distinct pages there are. A homepage plus a contact page is not the same as a page for every service, each with its own copy and SEO. Every different page layout is design and development work.
- How it's built. A builder like Wix, WordPress with a bought theme, or a custom-built site have very different setup and running costs. I compare them in [custom website vs WordPress](/en/blog/web-a-medida-vs-wordpress).
- Original design or an adapted one. Tweaking a template to your colours is quick. Designing from scratch for your brand takes longer.
- Features. A contact form is simple. A booking system, a private area or a site in two languages add up quickly.
- Who supplies the copy and photos. If you bring them, great. If they need writing or a photo shoot, that's paid for too (and usually worth it).
- Baseline SEO. Page titles, structured data, speed. Some quotes include it, others leave it for "later", which usually means never.

That's why two quotes with the same label ("business website") can have nothing in common. One includes ten pages with SEO, the other three pages on an untouched template.

## The costs that show up after launch

This is the part that surprises people most. You don't pay for a website once and walk away. There are recurring costs worth knowing before you sign:

- Domain: renewed every year. Cheap, but if it lapses, your site and your email go with it.
- Hosting: where the site lives. It ranges from very cheap plans to pricier managed services, depending on traffic and what the site needs.
- Email on your own domain, if it's a separate subscription.
- Licences: premium themes, paid plugins, booking tools. On WordPress these pile up without you noticing.
- Maintenance: updates, backups, small changes. You do it, someone else does it, or nobody does it (that last one is the expensive option).

My take: a site that's cheap to launch but carries a stack of annual licences can cost more over three years than one that's pricier upfront with no subscriptions. Do the maths over three years, not the first month.

## How to compare website quotes without surprises

Put the quotes side by side and check they answer the same things. These are the questions I'd ask:

- How many pages are included, and which ones?
- Will the domain and hosting be in my name? If the answer is "don't worry, I'll handle it", worry a little.
- What happens if I want to switch providers? Do I keep the code and the content?
- Can I edit opening hours, prices or photos myself, or is every change a new invoice?
- What will I pay each year, and who pays it?
- How many rounds of changes are included? What if I go over?
- Are basic SEO and the legal pages (privacy policy, cookies, legal notice) included?

If a quote can't answer half of these, it isn't cheaper. It's just less complete.

One more thing: be as wary of a suspiciously low price as of an inflated quote with no breakdown. A good quote can be read line by line, and you can tell what you're paying for in each one.

## Fixed price or hourly

Some people bill by the hour, others give a fixed price. Hourly is flexible, but you don't know the final bill. Fixed gives you certainty, in exchange for agreeing clearly on the scope first.
