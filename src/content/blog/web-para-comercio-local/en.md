You run a hardware shop, a bookshop or a clothes shop on your high street. Someone searches on their phone for what you sell and gets the out-of-town superstore, a giant online retailer and, with luck, you. They have marketing budgets and whole teams working on their websites. You have a counter and not much time. It looks like a lost fight, but a website for local shops doesn't compete on the same things as a chain's. It competes on what the chain can't offer.

## What the chain has and what you have

It's worth being realistic about both columns.

The chain has an endless catalogue, aggressive prices, home delivery and a technically flawless website. You won't win there, and you don't need to.

You have things a chain can't copy:

- You're close. For someone who needs something today, ten minutes' walk away, that matters more than price.
- You know your stuff. You can advise, repair, order in, adjust. The chain gives you an aisle and a price tag.
- You have a name and a face. People go back to the shops where they're known.
- You can move fast. If something new comes in tomorrow, you can tell people today.

An independent shop's website has to tell exactly that story. If your site looks like a smaller copy of a chain's, you lose. If it shows what only you do, you're playing a different game.

## A website for local shops that gets found

First, you need to show up when someone in your area searches for what you sell. The playing field here is more level than it looks, because for searches with local intent Google weighs proximity and relevance heavily, not just brand size.

The basics to compete:

- A complete Google Business Profile: the right category, real opening hours, photos of the shop and a link to your website.
- A page for each important product line or service. "Clothing alterations", "keys and locks", "books ordered in". Each one is a search that could bring someone to you.
- Your website saying which town and neighbourhood you're in, in the title and in the text.

I explain all this in more detail in [local SEO for small businesses](/en/blog/seo-local-negocios), which is close to required reading if you have a physical shop.

## Getting chosen: what the website needs to say

Once they find you, the website has to give them a reason. You don't need to invent a slogan. Just describe what you already do, properly:

- Which brands or products you stock, without listing the whole catalogue.
- Which services you offer that the chain doesn't: advice, repairs, special orders, reserving items.
- Who's behind the counter. A photo of you or your team says more than any copy.
- Customer reviews, linked to your Google profile.

Ever seen an independent retailer website that just says "Welcome, we are a leading company in the sector"? It says nothing. People want to know why they should come to your shop and not another one.

## Getting visits: the website as your shop door

The end goal of a local shop's website is usually to get someone through the door. So make it easy:

- Address with a map and directions, plus parking if there is any.
- Up-to-date opening hours, bank holidays included.
- Phone and WhatsApp tappable on mobile, so people can ask "have you got it?" before coming in.
- If you can, a way to reserve an item for in-store pickup. It's simple and people love it.

## What about selling online?

Here's my opinion, and someone could argue with it: you don't have to sell online from day one. An online store needs an up-to-date catalogue, photos of every product, synced stock, shipping and returns. For many independent shops that's more work than it's worth at the start.

Better to begin with a website that makes you visible and brings people into the shop, then look at the data to decide whether the jump makes sense. When the time comes, [how to set up an online store](/en/blog/como-montar-tienda-online) covers what it really involves.
