When I started working with Ū Medicina Estética, the clinic had just opened in Santander and was starting from zero: no website, with appointments and patient contact spread across Instagram, WhatsApp and paper. They wanted to get that in order and have a serious online presence. That's where I learned what sets a website for clinics apart from almost any other business: before booking, people need to trust you. Trust the person who'll treat them, what's going to be done, and what will happen to their data.

What follows is how I'd structure a clinic website (medical, dental, physio or aesthetic), using what we did at Ū as a real example.

## A website for clinics: one treatment, one page

The most common mistake on a clinic website is a "Services" page listing twenty treatments with two lines each. Someone looking for a specific treatment wants to know what it involves, whether it hurts, how long it takes, how many sessions they'll need and what aftercare looks like. Google wants the same before it shows you to someone searching for it.

At Ū, treatments and medical units are browsable sections, with a page for each treatment. So someone searching for a specific treatment lands right on it, not on a generic homepage.

## The team, with names and faces

At a clinic, people don't choose a building: they choose a practitioner. The team page is one of the most visited on any medical clinic website, so treat it seriously:

- A real photo of each practitioner, not a stock image.
- Full name, specialty and qualifications.
- Professional registration number where it applies. It's something a patient can check, and that's reassuring.

## Booking one click away from every page

If someone has read about a treatment and they're convinced, don't make them go back to the homepage to find your contact details. At Ū, the appointment request is one click away from every page on the site. It sounds like a detail. It's exactly the moment you win or lose a booking.

There are two ways to handle it. An appointment request (the patient suggests a time and the clinic confirms) or an online calendar where the patient picks a slot and it's booked. The second needs each practitioner's real availability in the system; the first is simpler to get started. Which one fits depends on how your clinic works, and I go into it in [online scheduling for clinics](/en/blog/agenda-online-clinicas). If you want to see how I build a custom appointment system, it's on [online booking systems](/en/services/online-booking-system).

For the clinic, what changes day to day sits behind the scenes: at Ū we built a custom back office where they manage the calendar, appointments, team and content, and appointment reminders go out automatically. The full case study is at [Ū Medicina Estética](/en/projects/umedicinaestetica).

## Aesthetic clinic website design: calm over flashy

With aesthetic clinic websites, there's a temptation to fill everything with effects, gradients and model shots. My view: the website should feel like the clinic does when you walk through the door. Clean, spacious, readable type and photos of the actual premises.

Before-and-after photos deserve a separate note. They work, but healthcare advertising has its own rules, and they vary by country and region. Before publishing any patient results, get written consent from each patient and check with your adviser. The same goes for anything that promises results.

## Data protection, built in from the start

This is the part nobody sees, and the part that matters most. At a clinic, any patient detail is health data, and data protection law (GDPR in the EU) treats it as a special category: more obligations, more care and heavier penalties if something goes wrong.

That shapes the entire site:

- Forms only ask for what's needed. Booking an appointment doesn't require a medical history.
- Consent is explicit and separate, never a pre-ticked box.
- Data is stored with providers that offer proper guarantees (in the EU, if that's where you are), with a data processing agreement signed.
- You can tell who accessed what. Ū has an access log for exactly that reason.

That's why I'm wary of the quick fix for clinics, WordPress with one plugin for appointments and another for forms: every plugin is one more door to watch, and with health data a leak isn't just a scare. I go into more detail in [GDPR for bookings and customer data](/en/blog/rgpd-reservas-datos-clientes). And have a professional review the legal pages; I build the technical side so it complies, I don't play lawyer.

## Where I'd start

Before thinking about colours, list the treatments you want people to find on Google and the practitioners with their photo and qualifications. Those two take the longest to get ready, and they're what builds the most trust.
