Open the legal notice on almost any small business website, read it carefully and you'll sometimes find another company's name. Or an address in a different city. Or a "[COMPANY NAME]" placeholder nobody replaced. That's what happens when legal pages are copied from another site just to tick the box, and it's the most common mistake I see with website legal requirements under GDPR. It's not the only one.

A fair warning first: I'm not a lawyer. I build the technical side so a website complies (the cookie banner actually works, forms give the right information, nothing loads before it should), but the text itself needs reviewing by a professional. What follows is so you know what to ask for and what to check, not a replacement for that adviser. I'll focus on the EU, where I work; if you're elsewhere, the principles are similar but the details change.

## Website legal requirements under GDPR: the mistakes I see most

I'm starting here because it's the most useful part. If your site has any of these, you already have a to-do list:

- Legal text copied from another website, with details that aren't yours.
- A cookie banner with a big "Accept" button and the reject option hidden on a second screen, or missing altogether.
- Google Analytics, ad pixels or embedded videos loading before the visitor has said anything.
- Contact forms without a line explaining who handles the data and why.
- A pre-ticked "I agree to receive marketing" box.
- A privacy policy that mentions services the site no longer uses (and leaves out the ones it does).

## The legal notice: who's behind the website

Many EU countries require any business offering services online to make it easy to find out who's behind the website. In Spain it's the "aviso legal" under the LSSI; in Germany it's the well-known Impressum. The content varies by country, but it typically includes:

- Business or company name and tax ID.
- Registered address and a contact email.
- Company registration details, if you're a limited company.
- For regulated professions: the professional body, registration number and qualification.
- Any licences your activity requires.

It isn't a long page. But it has to be yours and up to date.

## A privacy policy for a small business website

If your site collects any personal data (a contact form, a booking, a newsletter sign-up), data protection law (GDPR in the EU) requires you to explain clearly who the controller is, what the data is used for and on what legal basis, who it's shared with, how long it's kept and how people exercise their rights, including the right to complain to the data protection authority.

Two practical points:

- Information works in two layers: a short summary next to the form and a link to the full policy.
- The policy has to describe what your site actually does. If you use an email provider, a CRM or a booking tool, they belong in it.

If you also handle bookings or customer records, things get a bit more involved, and I cover that separately in [GDPR for bookings and customer data](/en/blog/rgpd-reservas-datos-clientes).

## A cookie consent banner that complies (and one that doesn't)

In the EU, cookies fall under the ePrivacy rules, applied through each country's own law, and national regulators' guidance makes several things fairly clear:

- Strictly necessary cookies (the ones the site needs to work, like a session or a basket) don't need consent.
- Analytics, advertising and social media cookies do. And they can't load until the visitor agrees.
- Rejecting has to be as easy as accepting. In practice, that means a reject button on the first layer of the banner, in plain sight.
- Visitors must be able to change their mind later, through a link that's always available.

This is where most websites fall down, and it's rarely the wording. It's the implementation: the banner says it respects your choice, but the code loads Google Analytics anyway. That part is my job, and it's the first thing I check on any site I take over. My opinion: if you can, use fewer cookies. A site without ad pixels or intrusive analytics needs a much simpler banner (or none at all, if it only uses strictly necessary cookies).

## Other pages you might need

Depending on your business:

- Terms and conditions, if you sell online or take paid bookings.
- A returns policy, if you have a shop.
- Specific consents if you handle health data, as a clinic does. The bar goes up considerably there.

## Where I'd start

Open your website in a private window and check three things: whether the legal notice has your details, whether the cookie banner lets you reject with one click, and whether there's an information line next to every form. If anything fails, ask your adviser for the text and whoever maintains the site to sort the technical side.
