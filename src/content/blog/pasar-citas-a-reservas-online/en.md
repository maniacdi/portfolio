It's nine in the evening, you're locking up, and there are four unanswered WhatsApp messages asking for an appointment, a missed call and a paper diary with two crossings-out on Thursday. You'll reply to everyone tomorrow, but by then one of them will have booked somewhere else, and another will tell you that you gave them the same slot as their neighbour. Sound familiar? Learning how to take bookings online is about breaking that loop: customers see the free slots and book on their own, whenever suits them, and you have the whole diary in one place.

In this guide I'll go through what you gain, what options there are, and how to make the switch without losing your regulars along the way.

## What changes when customers book themselves

The obvious gain is time. Every booking by phone or WhatsApp is a small negotiation: "Have you got Tuesday?", "Not in the afternoon", "What about Wednesday at five?". Multiply that by every appointment in a week and it's a good chunk of time you're not serving anyone.

But some less obvious things matter just as much:

- Bookings outside opening hours. Plenty of people remember to book at night or on a Sunday, exactly when you're not answering. With online booking for your business, that appointment still comes in.
- A single source of truth. If bookings arrive through three channels and get written down in two places, double bookings are only a matter of time.
- Automatic reminders. The system sends the confirmation and a reminder the day before without you lifting a finger. It's the simplest fix for no-shows, and I've written a whole post on [how to reduce appointment no-shows](/en/blog/reducir-citas-no-presentadas).
- Data. You know which services get booked most, which slots stay empty and which customers come back. Nobody knows that from a paper diary.

What doesn't change: some customers will keep phoning. That's fine. The point is for the phone to be one way in, not the only one.

## Before choosing a tool, write down your rules

The classic mistake is picking the tool first and finding out two weeks later that it doesn't fit how you work. Before you look at anything, jot down:

- Which services you offer and how long each takes. A haircut isn't a colour, and a check-up isn't a treatment.
- Who handles each service. If there are several of you on different hours, the diary gets a lot more complicated (I cover that in [scheduling multiple staff](/en/blog/agenda-varios-profesionales)).
- Whether you need a gap between appointments to clean, set up the room or just breathe.
- How far ahead people can book and how late they can cancel.
- Whether you'll ask for payment or a deposit at booking.

With those answers, half the decision is made. The other half is choosing between three routes.

## Three ways to offer online appointment scheduling

### Booking platforms

Calendly, Booksy, Treatwell, Fresha, TheFork for restaurants… You sign up, set your services and hours, and within an afternoon you have a booking link. Several of them also give you visibility inside their own app or marketplace.

Pricing usually comes in two shapes, sometimes combined: a monthly fee or a commission per booking (especially for customers they send you through their app). And there's a less visible cost: the customer books on their site, under their brand, and part of the relationship stays with the platform.

For a business that's starting out, or with a simple diary, online appointment scheduling for small business through a platform is usually the sensible choice. If that's you, I'll tell you so.

### Shopify, or a tool you already pay for

If you already sell on Shopify, there are booking apps that plug into your store and let you sell appointments as if they were products. That makes sense when services go together with products (a workshop with materials included, a session bundled with a kit), because payments and customers end up in one place. If you don't have a store, setting up Shopify just to take bookings is overkill.

Same with other software: some point-of-sale and business management tools already include an appointments module. Before adding anything, check what you're already paying for.

### Your own booking system

A custom build, integrated into your website: your services, your rules, your dashboard and your data. That's what I built for Ū Medicina Estética, a clinic in Santander, Spain: a bilingual website plus its own admin panel with the diary, appointments and patient records, with confirmation and reminder emails that go out automatically.

It pays off when a platform charges you commission on every booking and the volume starts to show on the bill, when your rules don't fit a generic tool, or when you want your customers' data in your own house rather than someone else's. In return, the upfront investment is higher and someone has to maintain it. With the clinic there was health data involved too, which weighs heavily when deciding where that data lives.

Every sector has its quirks. For restaurants the dilemma is almost always the per-cover commission, and I compare the options in [restaurant reservations: platform or your own system](/en/blog/reservas-restaurante-plataforma-o-propio). If you run a clinic, [online scheduling for clinics](/en/blog/agenda-online-clinicas) goes into what that sector needs specifically.

## How to take bookings online without losing customers

Here there really is an order, and skipping steps is what makes the transition go badly.

1. Move what's already in the diary first. Every future appointment goes into the new system before you open online booking. Otherwise, on day one someone books a slot that was already taken.
2. Open online booking without dropping the phone. For a few weeks everything runs side by side, with one rule: bookings that come in by phone or WhatsApp go into the system, entered by you, never into the paper diary. **One diary, even if bookings arrive through three doors.**
3. Put the link where people already look for you. A visible "Book" button on your website (at the top, not buried in the menu), the appointment link on your Google Business Profile, your Instagram bio and your WhatsApp Business auto-reply. That last one redirects the most people with the least effort: "Hi! You can book here, any time."
4. Decide whether to hold slots back. Some businesses keep phone-only slots at first, for older customers or special cases. That's reasonable while people get used to it.
5. Review after a few weeks. How many bookings come in online, which questions keep coming up, whether people abandon the form halfway. A booking form with twelve fields scares people off: name, phone or email and the service are usually enough.

## Should you take payment at booking?

It's the next question everyone asks. Asking for payment or a deposit cuts no-shows but adds friction: some people won't book if you want their card for a haircut. It makes more sense for long, expensive or limited-capacity services, and less for short appointments with loyal regulars. I go into it, including how to do it securely, in [taking a deposit on bookings](/en/blog/cobrar-senal-reserva-online).

## What I'd do in your shoes

If you're starting out or your diary is simple: a well-configured platform, with the link everywhere. You don't need more. If you're already losing a chunk to commission every month, have rules no tool can handle, or deal with sensitive data, that's when I'd sit down and look at your own system.

What I wouldn't do is stay with the paper diary out of fear that customers won't adapt. They adapt faster than you think: most of them already book this way at the hairdresser's, the dentist's or their favourite restaurant.
