It's quarter to nine, two people are standing on the pavement with their phones out and they want dinner now. They search, spot your restaurant on the map, tap through to the website… and a 12 MB PDF menu starts loading forever, with prices from two seasons ago. Next option. That's how fast a table gets lost, and it's why restaurant website design has very little in common with designing a site for an accountant: people check it standing up, in a hurry and hungry.

This post is about what that website needs and where to put it. How to get it showing up on Google is a separate story, which I cover in [SEO for restaurants](/en/blog/seo-para-restaurantes).

## Restaurant website design starts with what diners look for

Think about how you pick a restaurant yourself. The questions are always the same, and almost in this order: is it open now? What do they serve and at what price? What does it look like? Can I book? How do I get there? Your website has to answer all five without anyone having to hunt.

In practice, that means the top of your homepage, on a phone and without scrolling, should show today's opening hours, a button to book (or call) and a way into the menu. Everything else can go further down. Know the kind of restaurant site that opens with a full-screen video and a poetic line about local produce? Lovely. And the opening hours are in the footer.

## An online menu for restaurants: HTML, not PDF

This is where most restaurant sites fall down, and it's the page people look at most. Your menu should be real text on the website, not a PDF or a photo of the chalkboard:

- It reads well on a phone without pinching to zoom.
- It loads instantly, even with a weak signal.
- Google can read it, so if someone searches for "seafood rice in Santander" and you serve it, you can show up.
- It takes a minute to update when a price or a dish changes.

Allergens are best marked on each dish. Beyond being information you generally have to make available to customers, it's the first thing anyone with an intolerance checks. And if you do a set lunch menu, you should be able to change it every morning without calling anyone.

One detail people really appreciate: visible prices. A menu without prices breeds suspicion, even if you're an upmarket place.

## Bookings: the button that brings in the money

There are three ways to handle bookings on a restaurant website, and each one works depending on the case:

- A big, tappable phone number. Simple, and enough if you have few tables and someone to answer the phone.
- An external booking platform embedded in the site. Quick to set up, in exchange for commissions or fees and your customers' details living in their system.
- Your own booking system. More upfront investment, but no per-cover commission and your customers in your own database.

I go through them properly in [restaurant bookings: platform or your own system](/en/blog/reservas-restaurante-plataforma-o-propio). What makes no sense is hiding the booking button inside a dropdown menu. If you want to see how I approach a custom booking system, it's on [online booking systems](/en/services/online-booking-system).

## Real photos, even if they're not magazine-perfect

People decide with their eyes. But a stock photo of a dish you don't serve is obvious from a mile off and works against you. Better a few photos of your own: the dining room, the terrace, your best-selling dishes, the team. In good natural light, a recent phone goes a long way.

Watch the file size: huge, unoptimised photos are one of the most common reasons a restaurant website is slow. Properly compressed and in modern formats, they look the same and weigh a fraction.

## Restaurant website ideas you can skip

In the same honest spirit: some things turn up on every restaurant site and add almost nothing.

- Music that autoplays on arrival. Nobody wants their phone blaring on the bus.
- Loading animations that last several seconds.
- A blog with three posts from years ago.
- Long contact forms for something a phone call solves.

A short, fast, up-to-date site beats a complete but abandoned one.

## The basics, identical everywhere

Address with a map, phone number and opening hours. And they need to match your Google listing and any platform you appear on exactly. If Google says you're closed on Mondays and your website doesn't, someone is going to find the shutters down and never come back. Bank holidays and annual closures, updated in both places.

## Where I'd start

If your restaurant already has a website, try this: open it on your phone, on mobile data rather than WiFi, and time how long it takes to find today's hours, the menu and how to book. If it's more than ten seconds, that's your to-do list.
