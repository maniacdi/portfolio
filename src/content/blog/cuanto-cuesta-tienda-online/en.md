"So how much is this going to cost me?" It's the first question anyone who wants to sell online asks, and it almost always refers to launch day only. But how much an online store costs depends mostly on what comes afterwards: you pay to build it once and you pay to run it every month. If you only compare build quotes, you're comparing half the picture.

I'm not going to give you a figure, because without knowing what you sell I'd be making it up. I'll give you something more useful: the items that make up the cost and how to add them up for your case.

## The build: what you pay once

This is everything that has to be done before you sell your first product. What moves the ecommerce website cost the most:

- The starting platform. Adapting a Shopify theme doesn't cost the same as building a custom store. I compare them in [Shopify or a custom online store](/en/blog/shopify-o-tienda-a-medida).
- The design. A theme from the Shopify theme store adjusted to your brand, or a design of your own. Paid themes are a one-off cost; a bespoke one means hours of design and code.
- Loading the catalogue. Twenty products can be uploaded by hand in an afternoon. Two hundred with variants, photos and proper descriptions are days of work, or a script that imports them.
- Your own rules. If your business needs something the platform doesn't do (customer-specific prices, code-locked products, special shipping), that's development and gets quoted separately.
- The non-code part: photos, product copy and legal texts. Lots of people leave it out, and then it's what delays the launch.

If you've already asked for quotes for a website without a shop, you'll notice this comes in noticeably higher. What a brochure site costs is in [how much a business website costs](/en/blog/cuanto-cuesta-pagina-web-negocio); a store adds the catalogue, payments, shipping and more legal obligations.

## Online store maintenance cost, month by month

This is the part that tends to be left out of the budget.

The platform. On Shopify, the plan fee. On WooCommerce or a custom build, the hosting, which for a store needs to be decent.

Apps or extensions. Reviews, filters, translations, invoices, an Instagram feed… Each one on its own looks cheap, but they add up and they don't cancel themselves. Before installing one, work out what it adds up to over three years, not what it costs this month.

Payment fees. A percentage of every sale plus, sometimes, a fixed amount per transaction. It depends on the gateway and the payment method, and on Shopify there can be an extra fee if you don't use theirs. I go into detail in [payment gateways for online stores](/en/blog/pasarelas-de-pago-tienda-online).

Technical maintenance. Updates, theme changes, fixing what a new app breaks, adjusting the store when something on the platform changes. You can do it yourself, pay for a few hours a month, or pay as you go. What I wouldn't do is leave it out of the plan.

And the usual: domain, email on your own domain, and the time someone spends packing orders, which costs money too even if it never shows up on an invoice.

## How to work out what your online store costs in year one

Open a spreadsheet and add up:

1. The full build (including photos and copy if you're outsourcing them).
2. Twelve months of platform or hosting.
3. Twelve months of every app you'll use from the start.
4. Fees: your sales forecast for the year times your gateway's percentage, plus the fixed fee per order if there is one.
5. The technical maintenance you'll pay for, or the hours you'll put in yourself.

For year two, drop item 1 and add whatever you plan to build next. If the store is doing well, item 4 grows, and that's a good sign.

This exercise changes a lot of decisions. A custom store usually costs more to build and less in subscriptions, but it needs someone looking after the code. Shopify is cheaper to set up and charges you every month. Which one pays off? It depends on how much you sell and how much the store will change. That's why it's worth doing the sum over three years, not one.

## What I'd do before asking for a quote

Come with three things: how many products you'll sell at the start, whether you already have photos and copy, and whether your business has any special rule.
