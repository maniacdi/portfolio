You've had happy customers for years who tell you so at the counter, and on Google you have eleven reviews. The latest is eight months old and one star, from someone annoyed that you were closed on a bank holiday. The place across the road has two hundred. When people ask me how to get more Google reviews, the problem is rarely that customers aren't happy. It's that nobody asked them at the right moment.

Reviews count for local SEO (I cover that in my [local SEO guide](/en/blog/seo-local-negocios)), but they count even more in the customer's decision. This post is about how to ask, how to automate it and how to reply. I've organised it around the questions I'd ask myself if I ran a local business.

## Can I ask customers for reviews?

Yes, and you should. Google allows and encourages it: your profile gives you a direct link so people can leave a review without searching for you. What it doesn't allow:

- Offering anything in return: a discount, a prize draw, a free coffee. Even if the review is honest, the incentive invalidates it.
- Asking only the people you know will be positive (and steering unhappy ones to a private form). That's called review gating, and it's banned.
- Writing them yourself, or getting family or staff to. Or buying them.

Buying reviews doesn't just break Google's rules; it can have legal consequences too. EU consumer law treats posting or commissioning fake reviews as an unfair commercial practice, and other countries have similar rules. Google is also pretty good at spotting them, so you end up without the reviews and with a profile under suspicion.

## When is the best moment to ask?

Right after the customer gets what they came for. At a hairdresser's, when they pay. At a clinic, when they leave the appointment or that same afternoon. For a home service, when you finish the job and check it together.

A week later they've forgotten. Ask before you're done and it feels odd.

What works best is the simplest approach: ask in person ("if you liked it, a Google review really helps me") and give them something so they don't have to search. A QR code at the counter with a direct link to your profile, or the link in a message.

## Can it be automated?

Yes, and that's where you see the biggest difference. If you have an [online booking system](/en/services/online-booking-system), you already know who came in and when. The same system can send a message a few hours after the appointment with the review link. To every customer, unfiltered, which is exactly what Google asks for.

It's the same logic as the reminders that help [reduce no-shows](/en/blog/reducir-citas-no-presentadas): the system already has the data, you just use it at the right time. A few details:

- One message, no chasing. If they don't leave one, they don't.
- The customer must have agreed to hear from you, and the message has to relate to the service they received. Data protection law (GDPR in the EU) is not something to improvise with.
- A direct link to your profile, not to your website for them to go looking.

If your profile is still half-finished, it's worth taking time to [optimise your Google Business Profile](/en/blog/optimizar-perfil-empresa-google) before asking for reviews. Sending people to a listing with the wrong hours wastes the effort.

## How do I respond to negative reviews?

First, not while you're angry. Read it, close the app and reply the next day. The reply isn't really for the person who wrote it: it's for the hundred people who'll read it afterwards and want to know how you treat customers when something goes wrong.

What works when you respond to negative reviews:

- Thank them for taking the time, even if it stings.
- Acknowledge what did go wrong, if something did. No copy-pasted "we apologise for any inconvenience caused".
- Explain in one sentence what happened or what you've changed, without arguing point by point.
- Offer to continue the conversation privately, with a phone number or email.

What doesn't: sarcasm, sharing customer details ("you came in on Tuesday at 5pm and…") or, if you run a clinic, any health detail at all. Data protection gets very serious there.

If a review is fake or breaks the rules (insults, someone who was never a customer, a competitor), you can report it from your profile. Google doesn't always remove it, but sometimes it does. In the meantime, reply politely anyway.

## What about the positive ones?

Reply to those too. "Thanks, Marta, glad you liked the cut" is enough. No need to stuff keywords into the reply (it shows and doesn't help). Replying to all of them tells readers there's a real person behind the business.

## How I'd start getting more Google reviews

Grab the review link from your profile, print a QR code and put it where customers pay. For a month, ask every customer in person. Reply to every review within two days. That alone moves most businesses into a different league.
