You've got two proposals for your business website in front of you. One is from an agency with an office, a website full of client logos and a team with fancy job titles nobody can explain. The other is from one person who replied quickly and asked questions nobody else had asked. Which one do you go with? Freelance web developer vs agency is one of the most common dilemmas, and there's no single answer.

A heads-up before I start: I'm a freelancer, so I'm hardly neutral. Which is exactly why I'll try to be fair about what an agency does better. If you finish this thinking an agency is right for you, the post will still have done its job.

## What an agency does better

An agency has several people with different skills: design, development, copywriting, SEO, social media, ads. That has real advantages:

- Big projects. If you need a site with lots of sections, a complex shop, a launch campaign and photography, all at once and on a tight deadline, a team can split the work.
- Several services under one contract. If you also want them to handle social media, advertising or branding, one invoice keeps things simple.
- Continuity. If someone on the team goes on holiday or leaves, someone else can pick it up.

The usual downsides? It tends to cost more, because you're paying for overheads (office, management, sales); you often deal with an account manager rather than the person building your site; and on small projects you may not be a priority.

## What a freelancer does better

When you hire a freelance web developer, you're working with one person who does the job from start to finish. The advantages are real too:

- You talk to the person doing the work. Technical decisions are explained by whoever makes them, with no middlemen and no broken telephone.
- It's usually more affordable for the same result, because there are no overheads to cover.
- More flexibility. It's easier to shape the project around what you need rather than a fixed package.
- Commitment. Every project weighs on their reputation, and it shows.

And the limits, because there are some: limited capacity (one person can't run ten projects at once), fewer skills in one person (if you need photography or ad campaigns, they'll subcontract or refer you to someone) and the risk of depending on one specific person if something happens to them.

That last risk shrinks with one simple rule: everything in your name (domain, hosting, accounts), and the code and logins belong to you. That way you can switch to someone else without starting from zero. I go into more detail in [how not to depend on your developer](/en/blog/no-depender-de-tu-programador).

## Freelance web developer vs agency: my take

For a local business website (a clinic, a restaurant, a shop, a professional practice), a good freelancer is usually the most sensible choice. The project fits one person, you get direct contact and you skip the overheads. An agency makes more sense when the project is large, when you need many disciplines at once, or when you want to outsource all your marketing, not just the website.

What really matters isn't the format, it's the specific person or team. There are excellent agencies and freelancers who vanish halfway through a project. And vice versa.

## Questions to ask a web developer before hiring

These work for both. They're the questions I'd ask:

- Can you show me real, live projects similar to mine? Ask for working links, not screenshots.
- Who exactly will build my website? Will I be talking to that person?
- Is the quote fixed? What does it include and what doesn't it?
- How is it delivered: all at the end, or in phases I can see?
- Will the domain, hosting and accounts be in my name?
- What will I be able to edit myself, and what will I have to ask for?
- How quickly do you usually reply?
- What happens after launch if something breaks?

## How to compare proposals without getting lost

Put the proposals side by side and look at three things besides the price. Whether they understand your business (a generic proposal that would fit anyone is a bad sign). Whether they explain the reasoning behind their decisions in words you understand. And whether they give you options or a single take-it-or-leave-it package.
