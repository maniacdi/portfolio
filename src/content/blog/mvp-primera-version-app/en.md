The most expensive mistake when building an app is also the most common: spending a year on the complete version, with every feature that came up in the first meeting, only to find at launch that people use two of them. The other ten cost money, delayed the launch and now need maintaining. MVP app development exists to avoid exactly that.

## What a minimum viable product is (and isn't)

A minimum viable product is the smallest version of your app that genuinely solves the main problem, well enough for someone to use it and tell you what's missing.

Both words matter. Minimum: just enough, no extras. Viable: it works, it doesn't crash and you're not embarrassed to show it. An MVP that freezes or makes people jump through five odd steps teaches you nothing, because they give up before reaching the thing you wanted to test.

What an MVP isn't: a mock-up that doesn't work, a demo for investors, or "the full app with a worse design".

## What my own apps taught me

[IronLog](/en/projects/ironlog), my gym app, does one thing: log sets and show progress. No accounts, no server, no sync. Dropping the login and the backend removed half the complexity, and the app does exactly what I need. [Compras App](/en/projects/compras-app) did need a server, because its whole point is sharing the list in real time; there, the minimum already included a backend, and pretending otherwise would have made no sense.

The lesson from both: the minimum is set by the problem you're solving. Sometimes it's an app with no server; sometimes the server is the one essential piece. And an app you use every day shows you quickly what's surplus, because you're the first to feel every bit of friction.

## Deciding what goes into the first version of an app

Start by writing down the main journey: the user opens the app, does X and gets Y. Everything that's part of that journey goes in. Everything else goes through a three-question filter:

- If it's missing, does anyone stop using the app? If not, it waits.
- Can it be done by hand at first? A full admin panel can wait if, early on, you can enter the data yourself.
- Is it expensive to undo if I get it wrong? Data decisions (what gets stored, how users are identified) do need proper thought from day one, even if the feature that uses them comes later.

What nearly always waits: social login, chat, loyalty schemes, dark mode, multiple languages if your audience is local, advanced stats. What should never wait: keeping the data safe, the privacy policy and the app working properly on real phones.

## What to cut and what not to

Cutting features is fine. Cutting quality isn't. An MVP with a few well-built screens gives you real information. One with lots of half-finished screens gives you complaints.

I also wouldn't cut corners on what the stores look at: if it's going to the App Store and Google Play, review will check that it works, has a privacy policy and has no empty screens or broken links. An MVP doesn't get a pass on those rules.

## Measure to know what to build next

Launching the MVP is half the job. The other half is watching what happens:

- How many people use it more than once. If nobody comes back, the problem isn't the missing features.
- Where people get stuck. A screen where everyone drops off is a better clue than any survey.
- What they ask for. When three different people ask for the same thing without being prompted, you've got a candidate for version two.

All you need for this is simple in-app analytics (respecting privacy and declaring it in the store listing) and conversations with your first users. If they're your customers, even better: you already know where to find them.

## How much MVP app development saves

I won't give you numbers, because they depend on each app. But the logic is simple: every screen and integration you leave for later is money you don't spend until you know it's needed. And if it turns out it isn't, you never spend it. There's a breakdown of what makes an app more expensive in [how much it costs to make an app](/en/blog/cuanto-cuesta-hacer-una-app).

## Where I'd start with your app's MVP

Write the main journey in three lines and list everything else. Run it through the filter above. Whatever survives is your MVP, and it's usually a lot less than you expected.
