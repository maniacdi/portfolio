One Monday morning your website won't load and the company email has stopped receiving messages. You call the person who built the site years ago and they don't pick up. Eventually it turns out the domain expired, it was registered in their name, and the renewal reminders were going to an inbox nobody checks. This happens far more often than it should, and you avoid it by understanding three things: domain and hosting for a small business, plus the SSL certificate.

You don't need to know about servers. You need to know what each piece is, who owns it and what to ask. I'll go through it as questions and answers, since that's usually how the doubts arrive.

## What is a domain, and why must it be in your name?

The domain is your address on the internet: yourbusiness.com or yourbusiness.co.uk. You don't buy it forever: you rent it for a number of years from a registrar and renew it.

What matters isn't where you register it, but who's listed as the registrant. The registrant is the legal owner, and if that isn't you, in practice the domain isn't yours. If one day you want to switch provider or developer, you'll need the registrant to authorise the transfer.

What I'd check today:

- That the registrant is your company or you, with your details.
- That the domain's contact email is one you own and actually read.
- That you can log into the registrar account with your own username and password.
- That auto-renewal is switched on.

It's what we did with Ū Medicina Estética: every account in the clinic's name, with me as a collaborator with access. If I stopped working with them tomorrow, they wouldn't need to ask me for anything.

## And what's hosting? How is it different from the domain?

The domain is the address; hosting is the house. It's the server where your website's files live and where they're served from to whoever visits. They're separate things and can be with separate providers: the domain "points" to the hosting through records called DNS.

How to choose web hosting depends on the kind of site you have:

- Shared hosting: cheap and fine for a simple site with little traffic. You share a server with lots of other sites, and sometimes you feel it in the speed.
- Managed WordPress hosting: pricier, but they handle updates and backups. Makes sense if your site runs on WordPress and you don't want to wrestle with it.
- Platforms for modern sites (such as Vercel or Netlify): built for custom sites using frameworks like Next.js. Fast, with the certificate included. Ū's website, for example, is deployed on Vercel.
- Your own server or a VPS: full control, and full responsibility. For a normal business website, it's rarely worth it.

My view: for a local business website, speed and reliability matter more than the monthly hosting bill. Saving a few pounds a month on slow hosting costs you more in customers closing the tab.

## What is an SSL certificate, and do I need one?

An SSL certificate is what lets your site run on https, so the connection between the visitor and your website is encrypted. It's the padlock you see in the browser bar.

Yes, you need one. Without it, browsers flag your site as "Not secure", which scares anyone off, and if you have a contact form, the data travels unencrypted. Google takes it into account too.

The good news: free certificates exist today (Let's Encrypt is the best known) and most hosts install and renew them automatically. If someone charges you every year for "the SSL" on an ordinary business website, ask what exactly it includes. There are paid certificates with extra validation, but a brochure site rarely needs one.

What you do need to watch is renewal. Certificates expire, and if the automatic renewal fails, your site suddenly shows a full-screen security warning.

## Domain and hosting for a small business: what to ask before you sign

If someone is building your website, these questions will save you a scare:

- Whose name will the domain be registered in? Right answer: yours.
- Who pays for hosting, and whose name is the account in?
- Is the SSL certificate included, and does it renew automatically?
- What happens if I want to leave? Will you hand over the logins and the code?
- Who gets the expiry reminders?

If any answer is vague, ask for it in writing. I go further into how to avoid being tied to whoever built your site in [how not to depend on your developer](/en/blog/no-depender-de-tu-programador).
