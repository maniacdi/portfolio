You're closing for two weeks in August and want to put it on the website. You email whoever built it. They reply four days later, by which point customers have spent three days knocking on a locked door. Sound familiar? If changing your opening hours means waiting on someone else, the site will end up out of date, and an out-of-date website looks worse than no website. So being able to update your website without a developer isn't a nice-to-have: it decides whether your site is still alive a year from now.

## Updating your website without a developer: what you should be able to change

Not everything needs to be editable. If everything can be changed, everything can be broken. The sensible approach is to split things by how often they change.

What changes often, and what you should be able to edit yourself without asking anyone:

- Opening hours, bank holidays and notices ("closed for holidays from the 5th to the 19th").
- Prices and rates.
- Menu, daily specials or list of services.
- Gallery, team or product photos.
- News, articles or updates, if you publish them.
- Testimonials and reviews.

What rarely changes, and is fine to go through whoever maintains the site:

- Page structure and the menu.
- Design: colours, fonts, page layouts.
- New features: a different form, a booking system, another language.
- The technical side: baseline SEO, speed, security.

Where the line falls depends on each business and how hands-on you want to be. But if the whole first list depends on a developer, something has been set up wrong.

## Your options for editing business website content

There are several ways to give you control over that content. Here they are with their catch, because they all have one.

Site builders like Wix or Squarespace. You edit everything yourself, visually. That's their big strength and also their risk: the same mouse you use to change your hours can knock the whole design out of place. And the day you want to leave, the website stays behind.

WordPress. Editing text and posts is straightforward and widely known. In return, WordPress itself, the theme and the plugins all need keeping up to date. The full comparison is in [custom website vs WordPress](/en/blog/web-a-medida-vs-wordpress), so I won't repeat it here.

A headless CMS. The site is custom-built and the content is edited in a specialised external dashboard. It works well and keeps design and content separate, though it usually adds another account and sometimes another monthly fee. For a small business, a headless CMS is a solid middle ground if you want a custom site without a custom back office.

A custom back office. An admin panel built for your business, with exactly the fields you need: "price", "summer hours", "dish of the day". Nothing else. It's the option that takes the most work upfront, but it's also the hardest to break and the most comfortable day to day. I go into it in more detail in [custom admin panels](/en/blog/panel-de-gestion-a-medida).

## A real example: Ū Medicina Estética's back office

On the Ū Medicina Estética website, everything on the public side is edited by the clinic itself without touching code. From their back office they manage treatments, the team, testimonials, the homepage, notices and blog articles, using a text editor much like any word processor. That same back office also runs their calendar and appointments, so they don't have one tool for the website and another for daily operations.

What I value most about that approach? Each screen only has what's needed. When something is easy to update, it gets updated. When it's awkward, it gets left for "later".

## What to ask before commissioning a website

If you're about to commission a website, these questions tell you a lot about how dependent you'll be on whoever builds it:

- What will I be able to change myself, and what will I need to ask you for?
- Can you show me the admin panel before we start, or in a demo?
- If I want to change my hours on a Sunday night, how do I do it?
- How many users can there be, and with what permissions? (Handy if you want a staff member updating the menu without touching anything else.)
- What happens to my content if I switch provider? Can I export it?
- Can changes I make break the design or the SEO?

If the answer to the first question is "just email me for anything", think twice.

An extra tip for opening hours: keep your Google Business Profile up to date too. Lots of people check your hours there and never click through to the website, so it's the first place to change.
