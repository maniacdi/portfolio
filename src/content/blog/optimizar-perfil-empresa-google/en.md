You search for your own business on your phone, the listing pops up on the map, and the opening hours are last summer's, the main photo shows someone else's shopfront and the category says "Store". Sound familiar? Your Google Business Profile is the first thing many people see of you, before your website, and it's almost always half-done. Google Business Profile optimisation is mostly about finishing that job properly.

In my [local SEO guide](/en/blog/seo-local-negocios) I said that for a neighbourhood business the profile matters more than the website. Here I go into the detail: what to change, in what order and what to avoid. Just the profile; reviews get their own post.

## First things first: make sure the profile is yours

If your business already shows up on Google Maps, the profile probably exists even if you never created it (Google builds them from public data). Step one is claiming it with a Google account you control, not your nephew's or the one belonging to the agency that built your site five years ago. Google will ask you to verify it, sometimes with a short video of your premises or your work tools. It's a chore, but you only do it once.

If someone else needs to manage it, add them as a manager. Never hand over your password.

## Google Business Profile optimisation, piece by piece

This is the order I'd follow, from what matters most to what matters least.

### 1. The name, exactly as it is

The listing name should be your real business name, the one on your sign. Stuffing keywords into it ("Ana's Hair | Cheap Hairdresser Santander Centre") breaks Google's guidelines and can get your profile suspended. It works for a few weeks… until someone reports it.

### 2. The primary category

This is probably the setting with the biggest influence on which searches you appear for. Pick the most specific one available: "Physiotherapy clinic" rather than "Health centre", "Basque restaurant" rather than "Restaurant". Then add secondary categories only if you genuinely do those things. If you're unsure, look at which categories the businesses ranking above you in your area use.

### 3. Opening hours, including the odd ones

Regular hours, public holidays and holiday closures. Google often nudges you before bank holidays to confirm your hours; do it. Few things earn a one-star review faster than someone turning up to a locked door.

### 4. Service area or address

If customers come to you, use your exact address, written the same way as on your website. If you go to them (plumbing, renovations, photography), hide the address and set the towns or area you cover. That's what I do for my own business, since I don't have premises open to the public.

### 5. Services or products

The profile lets you list services with a description and, optionally, a price. Fill it in with the words a customer would use, not your internal jargon. It's text Google reads, and it helps relevance when someone searches for something very specific.

### 6. Real photos

Shopfront (so people recognise you when they arrive), interior, team and finished work. Taken on your phone in good light is fine. Stock photos are obvious and add nothing. Upload a new one now and then: a profile with years-old photos looks like a business that has closed.

### 7. Your website link and your booking link

Link to your website, and if a service page fits better than the homepage, consider pointing there. If you take bookings online, the profile lets you add a direct booking link. It's the shortest path between "found you" and "booked in".

## Posts and questions: the upkeep

Once the profile is set up, what keeps it alive is posting (updates, offers, events) and answering what people ask. You don't need to post every day. One post a month with something real, like a new service or your Christmas hours, already sets you apart from the abandoned profiles.

If your profile shows customer questions, answer them yourself before a stranger does with the wrong information. Google has been changing how that section works, so check what shows up in your case.

## What I wouldn't do

- Create a duplicate profile "for more visibility". Google merges or removes duplicates, and in the meantime they confuse everyone.
- Use a different tracking phone number everywhere without knowing what you're doing. Name, address and phone need to match your website.
- Listen to whoever calls offering to "verify your Google listing" for a fee. The profile is free, and so is verifying it.
