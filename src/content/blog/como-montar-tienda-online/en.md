Your physical shop is doing fine, you've got an Instagram full of product photos, and every week someone messages you asking whether you ship to the other end of the country. You say yes, send them your bank details, wait for the transfer, pack the parcel and jot the order down in a notebook. It sort of works. If you're wondering how to start an online store for your shop, this is the order I'd make the decisions in, along with the mistakes that cost the most when you fix them late.

I'm writing from what I built for [Ū Skin Store](/en/projects/uskinstore), the Shopify store of an aesthetic medicine clinic in Santander, and from [Showcase](/en/blog/showcase-plantilla-webs-comercios), the base I use for small business websites, which comes with its own demo shop.

## Before you start an online store: what you'll sell, and to whom

The first question isn't Shopify or WooCommerce. It's which part of your range makes sense to sell online. Some products travel badly, weigh too much for what they're worth, or need someone to recommend them. Starting with your whole inventory is usually a mistake: more product pages to maintain, more photos to take and more stock to reconcile between the counter and the website.

Think too about the rules you already apply in the shop without noticing. At Ū Skin Store, some products come from selective distribution brands, which don't allow them to be sold to just anyone in one click. In the clinic, the doctor handles that by recommending them in person; online, it had to be built, and I wrote about it in [hiding a product isn't protecting it](/en/blog/ocultar-el-producto-no-es-protegerlo). If your business has something similar (trade prices, minimum orders, products you can't ship to certain places), write it down now, because it shapes the platform.

## Where to build it: Shopify, WooCommerce or custom

To set up an online shop for a small business there are three sensible routes:

- Shopify: you pay a monthly fee and stop worrying about servers, payment security and updates. It's what Ū Skin Store runs on.
- WooCommerce: the WordPress shop plugin. Free to start with, but hosting, paid extensions and maintenance are on you.
- Custom: when the way you sell doesn't fit either of them, or the shop is part of something bigger, like a management dashboard or a booking system.

My take: for most shops starting to sell online, Shopify is the sensible choice, and whatever it doesn't do out of the box can be built on top. If you're unsure, there's a comparison in [Shopify or a custom online store](/en/blog/shopify-o-tienda-a-medida), and what each option costs in [how much an online store costs](/en/blog/cuanto-cuesta-tienda-online).

What I wouldn't do is choose based on the first month's price. What matters is what you pay every month for years: platform, apps, fees and whoever looks after it.

## The catalogue, the part nobody budgets for

This is where almost every store gets stuck, and it isn't the design. Each product needs a title, description, price, photos, weight, variants (sizes, colours, volumes) and, if you want filters to work, a brand and a category spelled exactly the same every time.

At Ū Skin Store the catalogue lives in a spreadsheet the clinic maintains, and Python scripts turn it into the Shopify import. Nobody adds products by hand in the store admin. It sounds like overkill for a small shop, but it has one huge advantage: **there's a single source of truth**. A product created by hand directly in Shopify exists and sells, but the next import doesn't know about it, so it never gets updated again.

Two things worth deciding early:

- How each brand and category is written. "ZO Skin Health" and "Zo Skin Health" are the same to you and two different brands to an automated collection.
- Which photos you'll use. Your own, with similar backgrounds and lighting, or the manufacturer's if they let you and the resolution is good. Mixing the two half-heartedly is what doesn't work.

If your catalogue is large or changes often, I go into more detail in [how to import and update your store catalogue automatically](/en/blog/importar-catalogo-tienda-online).

## Payments: the fewer surprises, the better

To take money you need a payment gateway, the service that processes the card and pays it into your account. On Shopify the usual choice is Shopify Payments, which is built in; you can connect others, but then Shopify generally charges an extra fee on every sale. With WooCommerce or a custom store you pick: Stripe, PayPal, your bank's card processing service…

What I'd look at: which methods your customers expect (cards, Apple Pay, PayPal, local options popular in your country), what each one costs per sale and how long the money takes to reach your account. I compare them in [payment gateways for online stores](/en/blog/pasarelas-de-pago-tienda-online).

And one rule with no exceptions: card details never touch your server. With a serious gateway you don't need them to, and you shouldn't.

## Shipping: where money leaks without you noticing

Shipping is the most boring part of selling online and the one that can eat the most margin. Courier rates usually go by weight bands, so the weight on each product page matters. If it's too low, the store charges the customer a cheaper band than the one you then pay, and you lose money on every order without any alarm going off.

At Ū Skin Store, when a product hadn't been weighed yet, I set the estimated weight on the high side. If I overshoot, the customer pays a bit more, it shows up on the first order and gets fixed. If I undershoot, nobody tells you.

Also decide:

- Whether you offer click and collect. For a shop with premises it's the easiest thing in the world, and plenty of people prefer it to waiting in for a courier.
- Whether you do free shipping over a certain amount, and whether your margins can take it.
- What happens with returns, and who pays for the return postage.

## The legal side of an online store

An online store has more obligations than a brochure website. In the EU, at a minimum: a legal notice with your business details, a privacy policy (data protection law, GDPR in the EU), a cookie policy, and terms of sale that state prices including tax, shipping costs, delivery times and how to exercise the right of withdrawal.

In the EU that right gives customers 14 days to return something bought at a distance without giving a reason, with specific exceptions (sealed hygiene products that have been opened, for example). And the final order button has to make it clear that clicking it means paying. Other countries have their own rules, so check the ones that apply where you sell.

Legal texts aren't something you copy from another store: they have to describe what you actually do. If in doubt, get someone who knows the subject to review them.

## What I'd do before opening

Place real test orders, with a real card, and refund them. Ship one to your own home. Check which discounts are active, especially if the store has had trials or campaigns before: a forgotten discount applying to the whole catalogue is the kind of thing that only shows up on a test order. Better you catch it than a customer.

And get the store ready to be found: product pages with your own descriptions, well-organised categories and a site that loads fast. I cover that in [ecommerce SEO](/en/blog/seo-tiendas-online).

## Where to start tomorrow

If I had to build your shop's online store today, I'd start with a spreadsheet: the products you want to sell first, with price, weight and a decent photo of each. With that you can choose the platform based on data rather than guesswork, and half the work that usually delays a store is already done.
