Monday, 9am. You open your booking software, export the week's appointments, paste them into a sheet, message every client to remind them and, while you're at it, update stock for what you sold on Saturday. An hour and a half that repeats every week without anyone ever deciding it should. If you want to automate business tasks, that's where to start: the things you do the same way every time.

These are automation examples I've built myself or see fitting almost any small business, grouped by type of task.

## Alerts that send themselves

This is the most satisfying kind. Something happens (or is about to) and someone needs to know.

In the admin panel I built for [Ū Medicina Estética](/en/projects/umedicinaestetica), appointment reminders go out on their own through scheduled jobs: nobody at the clinic has to remember to send them. On the [wedding website](/en/projects/boda-web) I made for a friend, when a guest leaves a message, the couple get an automatic email. Small things that take work off someone's plate every day.

More of the same kind:

- A reminder 24 hours before an appointment, by email or text. If no-shows cost you money, there's more in [how to reduce missed appointments](/en/blog/reducir-citas-no-presentadas).
- An internal alert when a product's stock drops below a minimum.
- An email to the customer when their order changes status.
- A ping to you when a contact form comes in asking for a big quote.

## Data that moves between tools on its own

Copy-pasting between tools is the most repeated task there is, and the one that introduces most errors. Every time someone types in something that already existed elsewhere, there's a chance they type it wrong.

- An online order that goes into your invoicing software without anyone retyping it.
- A customer who books and is automatically added to your client list.
- A supplier's catalogue (an Excel file or CSV) loaded into your shop instead of creating products one by one. I cover it in [how to import your catalogue into your online store](/en/blog/importar-catalogo-tienda-online).

The key here is whether your tools let you connect them. Many do, through native integrations or services like Zapier or Make; if not, a small custom piece is usually enough.

## Reports that turn up without asking

How much did you sell last week? Which service gets booked most? If answering means opening three programs and building a table, that table can land in your inbox every Monday instead. Same data, zero minutes of your time. And because it always comes out the same way, you can compare weeks without wondering whether you forgot a column this time.

## Documents that fill themselves in

Quotes, contracts, consent forms, invoices. If they always have the same structure and only the name, date and amount change, they can be generated from data you already have. Careful with documents containing personal data: whatever you automate has to comply with data protection law (GDPR in the EU) just as it would if done by hand, meaning who can access it, where it's stored and for how long.

## What about AI?

It can sort emails, summarise notes or draft a first reply. I use it every day, and I explain how in [how I use AI in my work](/en/blog/como-uso-la-ia-en-mi-trabajo). But my view is clear: for tasks that are always the same, a fixed rule is cheaper, more predictable and doesn't get things wrong with total confidence. Save AI for work that genuinely needs to interpret text, and have a person check anything important.

## Which one to automate first

Not everything is worth it. My rule of thumb is simple: frequency, times duration, times the cost of a mistake. A five-minute task done twenty times a day outweighs a one-hour report once a month. And if a mistyped figure can cost you a customer, move it up the list.

What I wouldn't automate: processes you're still changing every week (you'd just automate the chaos and make it faster), and tasks where the personal touch is what sells.

If you're unsure whether to solve it with existing tools or something built for you, [custom or off-the-shelf software](/en/blog/software-a-medida-vs-estandar) shows how to run the numbers.

## Where to start

For one week, write down everything you do exactly as you did it the day before, and how long it takes. Pick the one that eats the most hours and check whether your tools already have an option to do it automatically. They often do, and nobody has switched it on.
