"If most of my tables are booked by regulars, why am I paying an app a commission on every one?" Sooner or later, anyone running a restaurant that's full at weekends asks that question. It has an answer, but not the same one for everyone: it depends on where your diners come from and what you want to do with their data. Let's compare the two ways to run a restaurant reservation system: the platform you already know, or your own on your website.

## How booking platforms charge

Pricing models change and every platform has its own, so always check the current rates. But they tend to come in three shapes, sometimes combined:

- A monthly fee for the restaurant booking software, with more or fewer features depending on the plan.
- A commission per diner or per booking, especially for bookings that come through the platform's app or website.
- Extras: in-app promotions, featured placement, discounts that you end up funding.

The catch is in the second point. If the platform brings you new customers who wouldn't have found you otherwise, that commission is advertising and can be worth it. If it also charges for the regular who used to phone you and now books through the app because it's easier, you're paying for something you already had.

So the first step is to look at your own numbers: of last month's bookings, how many were new customers who came via the platform, and how many were people who already knew you?

## Your customers' data: who does it belong to?

This matters more than it seems. When someone books through a platform, they leave their details with the platform. You see the booking, but the relationship with that diner partly belongs to the platform too: it can show them other restaurants, send them its own promotions and, if you leave tomorrow, much of that history stays behind.

With your own system, the diner books on your website and the data sits in your database. You can see who comes back, who always comes in a group and who celebrates their birthday with you. And, with their consent, you can tell them about your seasonal menu without asking anyone's permission (consent for marketing, mind you, has to be separate from the booking and clearly explained).

## Platform vs commission-free restaurant bookings: side by side

| | Booking platform | Your own system |
|---|---|---|
| Getting started | Days, no development | Weeks, depending on what you need |
| Cost | Fee and/or commission, grows with volume | Upfront build plus maintenance, doesn't grow per booking |
| New customers | Visibility inside their app | Down to your website, Google and social media |
| Customer data | Shared with the platform | Yours |
| Custom rules | Whatever the tool allows | Whatever you need: sittings, tables, groups, set menus |

A platform gives you something your own system can't: a shop window. Your own system gives you what a platform has no interest in giving you: independence and a fixed cost.

## When I'd stick with the platform

- If you've just opened and nobody knows you yet. The app's shop window is worth more than the commission for a while.
- If you're in a very touristy area, where most diners will never look for your website.
- If you take few bookings and the monthly commission is a small number. Building anything to save that isn't worth it.

## When your own restaurant reservation system pays off

- When most bookings come from people who already know you and the commission has turned into a fixed toll.
- When your rules don't fit the tool: two sittings with different times depending on the day, tables pushed together for groups, tasting menus with limited seats, or bookings that need advance notice.
- When you want your own customer base so you can build loyalty without a middleman.

There's a middle ground that's sometimes the most sensible option: keep the platform as a way to reach new diners, and put your own booking button on your website, Google profile and Instagram. People who discover you come in through the app; people who already know you book directly with you. For that to work, the two diaries have to stay in sync, or you'll have double-booked tables at ten on a Saturday.

Pull your bookings from the last three months and split them into two piles: new diners who came via the platform, and customers you already had. With that count in front of you, the decision almost makes itself.
