It's ten past eleven, the appointment was at eleven and the chair is still empty. You call and hear "Oh, sorry, it completely slipped my mind". No bad intent: they forgot. That's actually good news, because forgetting is cheap to fix. Automated appointment reminders are the first line of defence against no-shows, but not the only one, and it's worth applying the measures in order: from the one that bothers customers least to the one that bothers them most.

## Why people don't turn up

Before reaching for fixes, it's worth separating the reasons, because each one calls for something different:

- They forgot. The most common, and the easiest to solve.
- Something came up and they didn't let you know because cancelling was a hassle: you had to phone during opening hours, nobody picked up, they felt awkward.
- They booked "just in case", without much commitment, because booking cost them nothing.

The first one is fixed with reminders. The second, by making cancelling easy. The third is the only one that justifies asking for money upfront.

## Appointment confirmation emails: the first reminder

Right after booking, an email with the day, time, address and a button to add it to their phone's calendar. It sounds like nothing, but once the appointment is in the customer's calendar, their own phone will remind them. It's free and works without you doing anything.

## Automated appointment reminders, at the right time

The classic is a reminder the day before. For services booked weeks in advance, an extra one a few days earlier helps, because it leaves time to fill the slot if the customer can't make it.

As for the channel, each has its trade-off:

- Email: free and unlimited, but read less often, and it sometimes ends up in promotions or spam. Perfect for the confirmation.
- SMS: almost always read, but every message costs money and the text is short.
- WhatsApp: the most-checked of all, but sending automated messages requires the WhatsApp Business API, with templates approved by Meta and per-message charges. It isn't a case of adding a number and you're done.

My take: email for the confirmation and the reminder, always, and SMS or WhatsApp only if your no-shows justify the cost. Start with the cheap option and measure.

One thing about the content: a reminder isn't advertising. It's a message about a service the customer asked for, so don't stuff offers or newsletters into it; those go separately, with their consent.

## Make cancelling easier than not turning up

This is the step people forget most. **If cancelling means phoning during office hours, a lot of people will simply not show up.** Put a link in every reminder to cancel or reschedule in two taps. That way the slot goes back into the diary and someone else can take it.

Go one step further and ask for active confirmation: "Coming tomorrow? Confirm / Cancel". Anyone who doesn't confirm gives you a hint to call them, or to line up someone from a waiting list. And if you have a waiting list, let the system notify the next person automatically when a slot frees up.

## A clear cancellation policy

A simple policy, visible when booking and repeated in the confirmation: how late people can cancel for free and what happens if they don't turn up. It doesn't need to be aggressive. Sometimes just having one, and having people read it, makes them take the appointment more seriously.

What doesn't work is a policy you never enforce. If you say you charge for no-shows and never do, your regulars know.

## Deposits: only when the rest isn't enough

Taking a deposit at booking is the most effective fix for "just in case" bookings, but it also adds the most friction. Some people won't book if you ask for their card. It makes sense for long, expensive or limited-capacity services, or for customers who've missed appointments before. ## To reduce no-shows, start by measuring

Before changing anything, count: how many no-shows you get a month, for which services, at what times, and whether it's always the same customers. Without that number you won't know if the reminders are working. With a booking system the figure comes out on its own; with a paper diary you'd have to write it down by hand (and nobody does).

## Where I'd start

This week: a confirmation email with a calendar button, and a reminder the day before with a cancel link. Measure for a couple of months. If no-shows still hurt on a particular service, that's when I'd look at deposits or SMS.
